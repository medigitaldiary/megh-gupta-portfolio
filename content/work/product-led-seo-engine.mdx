<Section variant="narrative" eyebrow="The problem" heading="SEO couldn't scale on content alone">

Performance ads were doing the acquisition work. Expensive, non-compounding, tied to monthly spend.

A content-only approach would have hit three structural problems. Google couldn't discover most of the site (crawlability). Publishing was gated behind SEBI review (bottleneck). And there was no mechanism for SEO to enter engineering tickets as a requirement (governance).

Hiring more writers wouldn't fix any of those.

<Callout variant="insight">
SEO had to be built as a product workstream, not a marketing function. Three problems, three different kinds of fix.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section variant="narrative" eyebrow="The strategy" heading="Five connected workstreams">

The strategy sat on five pillars, each of which needed the others to work.

<StepGrid variant="parallel" numbered>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Technical foundations" eyebrow="Workstream">
    Mapped the site graph, fixed crawlability, shipped schema and sitemap. The whole engine fails if Google can't see the site.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Self-serve publishing" eyebrow="Workstream">
    Built an AI compliance agent on top of Strapi so blogs ship at 4-a-day without a manual review queue.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Governance" eyebrow="Workstream">
    Made SEO requirements part of the PRD, review, and verify stages for every product ticket.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Editorial content" eyebrow="Workstream">
    350+ compliance-reviewed blogs targeting the keywords retail bond investors actually search.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Programmatic depth" eyebrow="Workstream">
    26,000+ ISIN-level pages + 200+ Bond Dictionary entries for long-tail and definitional queries.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
</StepGrid>

<Callout variant="insight">
Programmatic alone would've looked like thin content. Dictionary alone wouldn't scale. Editorial alone wouldn't compound fast enough. The three together, held up by foundations and governance, is what made it a moat.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section variant="deepDive" eyebrow="Workstream 01" heading="Understanding the site graph">

Before anything shipped, I ran a crawl of all 372 blog posts and mapped internal linking across them. The point was to see what Google would actually see.

The structural finding: the hub and archive pages used JavaScript-only pagination, which Google can't crawl. 230+ posts were invisible. 67% of blog posts were orphaned.

What shipped to fix it:

- Static archive pagination (replaced the JS-only pagination).
- Footer sitemap links to hub pages (short path from homepage to every post).
- schema.org markup across page types.
- A proper sitemap.xml architecture + a sitemap directory page.
- GSC set up from scratch; llms.txt for AI crawlers.

<Callout variant="result">
Internal linking depth improved ~50–60% after the fixes shipped. That's a structural measure of how reachable the catalog is to a crawler, not a traffic number, the traffic effect compounded over the following weeks.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section variant="deepDive" eyebrow="Workstream 02" heading="Removing manual bottlenecks">

Every public word on a bond platform has to clear compliance review. That isn't a nice-to-have on an OBPP. SEBI's rules on claims, disclaimers, and factual constraints apply to every blog, every landing page, every programmatic field label.

At 4 blogs a day, manual review was the ceiling. Writers queued, reviewers saturated, the publishing calendar slipped.

<StepGrid variant="compare">
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Manual compliance review" eyebrow="Before">
    Each blog waited 2–3 days in the reviewer's queue. A SEBI-sensitive line would come back marked for a rewrite; the writer rewrote; the blog went back to the queue. Four blogs a day was aspirational.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="Claude compliance agent + Strapi auto-publish" eyebrow="After">
    A compliance agent trained on SEBI OBPP guidelines reviews each blog before publish, flags lines that need rewriting, and lets clean copy move to Strapi for scheduled publication. Four blogs a day became the sustained cadence.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
</StepGrid>

What the Claude agent actually does. It reads the blog draft against a corpus of SEBI compliance rules, prohibited claims, mandatory disclaimers, factual constraints, and returns a line-level diff with suggested rewrites, not a pass/fail. Final sign-off stays with Rehana on anything the agent flags as ambiguous.

I don't have verified CMS time-savings numbers, so I'm not claiming any. What I can claim is the publishing cadence we hit, which we couldn't hit before.

</Section>

<Section variant="narrative" eyebrow="Workstream 03" heading="Making SEO part of product delivery">

Before this, SEO lived with marketing. Product tickets got sized without SEO requirements, and shipped features would quietly break canonical URLs, pagination, or crawl paths. The fix wasn't a new team, it was putting SEO into the three gates every product ticket already goes through.

<StepGrid variant="sequence">
  <StepGrid.Cell title="SEO requirements enter the PRD" eyebrow="Specify">
    Every product ticket's PRD carries an SEO section: canonical URL pattern, pagination behavior, sitemap entry, schema markup, meta/OG. If the section is blank, the ticket isn't sized.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="SEO is a line-item in design and tech review" eyebrow="Review">
    Design review flags any pattern that would hide content from a crawler. Tech review flags any route that would 404, 302, or render client-side-only.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
  <StepGrid.Cell title="QA includes an SEO pass" eyebrow="Verify">
    View-source check for canonical, OG, JSON-LD, discovery block. Rich Results Test on the live preview URL. Sitemap updated in the same PR, or the PR doesn't merge.
  </StepGrid.Cell>
</StepGrid>

SEO stopped being a thing marketing asked for after the fact. Engineering started catching their own breakages at review time.

</Section>

<Section variant="narrative" eyebrow="What we stopped" heading="The Framer landing pages we stopped shipping">

We tried shipping fast-iteration landing pages in Framer. Marketing could spin up a page in an afternoon; the design felt crisper than our own stack; the publishing loop felt right.

Two things broke it. Framer pages lived outside the compliance review path. And they used client-side rendering in ways our main stack had just finished moving away from, the exact mistake the technical foundations work was meant to fix. We killed the experiment after three landing pages.

<Callout variant="caution">
The lesson wasn't that Framer is wrong. It was that quality gates, compliance, crawlability, canonical, belong upstream of the publishing tool, not inside it. A tool that lets you ship around the gates isn't a faster tool; it's a slower one with the slowness hidden until the next quarter.
</Callout>

</Section>

<Section variant="reflection" eyebrow="Reflection" heading="What I'd improve next">

Three things I'd do differently if I were starting again.

- **Data freshness.** Some ISIN pages carry rating data months old. Not an SEO problem yet, but it will be once Google's quality signals catch up. A page-level freshness refetch policy belongs in v1, not v2.
- **Per-page instrumentation.** We flew on headline metrics for the first two months. Crawl budget per page type and click yield per page would have told us earlier which pillar to double down on.
- **Governance for experiments.** The Framer episode was one exception that slipped through. A "compliance + crawlability acceptance" checklist for every new publishing surface, baked into Specify-Review-Verify, would catch the next one before it ships.

<Callout variant="learning">
Each one of these isn't a mistake I made. They are the things I'd build earlier, the kind of "earlier is cheaper" learnings you can only see once the engine is actually running.
</Callout>
